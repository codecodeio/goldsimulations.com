Research Spotlight: A Blueprint for Online Economics Simulations&#x20;

In a 2008 conference paper, Dr. **Steven Gold (Rochester Institute of Technology)** outlines a design approach that still feels modern: pair a market-structure simulation with **automated coaching** (so students get timely guidance) and **graded exercises** (so instructors can assess learning directly).

[**Read the full paper (PDF)**](https://repository.rit.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1661\&context=other)

---

## What the paper is trying to solve

The paper starts from two practical questions instructors still ask today:

1. *Do simulations actually improve learning?*
2. *How can we tell what students learned—beyond who “won” the game?*

Dr. Steven Gold argues that many business sims miss two “in-the-moment” supports that matter in real classrooms:

* **Coaching/feedback during play**, when students are confused and the instructor can’t realistically coach everyone at once.
* **Direct assessment inside the sim**, so instructors can measure understanding of specific concepts—not just overall performance outcomes.

---

## The simulation example: *Beat the Market (BTM) Online*

To demonstrate the idea, the paper describes **Beat the Market (BTM) Online**, a micro/managerial economics game built around the four core market structures:

* **Perfect competition**
* **Monopoly**
* **Monopolistic competition**
* **Oligopoly**

Students run a firm over time by entering decisions each period and advancing the simulation to the next round.

### Instructor-flexible decision set (up to 8 levels)

BTM supports multiple decision “dials” that an instructor can enable/disable depending on course goals, including:

* price, production, plant size
* advertising, product development, e-commerce
* process improvement, training

Performance is reported as a rating (0–100%) based on profits relative to the best-performing firm.

---

## Two standout features: the “consultant” + auto-graded exercises

### 1) Automated consultant for coaching

After each round, the game provides a summary page that highlights performance and includes advice from an automated “consultant.”

In the paper’s example, the consultant flags specific signals like:

* marginal cost being well below price
* revenues declining from the prior period

A key design constraint: the consultant **should not simply tell students what to do**, because that undermines learning-by-doing. The coaching should guide attention and thinking—not hand over the “answer.”

### 2) Automatically graded exercises for assessment

To address the limits of “profit = learning,” the paper adds **graded exercises** tied to specific topics. The structure is simple:

1. the student plays a specific (assigned) game instance
2. the student answers multiple-choice questions about the results
3. the system **auto-grades** and sends results to an online gradebook

One clever anti-cheating / anti-copying choice: **each student’s game is algorithmically generated**, so environments differ across students while staying comparable in difficulty.

The paper also frames simulation performance as a way to capture higher-order skills (application, analysis, synthesis, evaluation), aligning with Bloom-style learning goals.

---

## Why this matters

Even though this paper is from 2008, it lays out a design pattern that maps neatly onto what modern instructors need:

* **Immediate feedback** when students are stuck (without requiring 1:1 instructor time)
* **Concept-level checks for understanding** embedded directly in gameplay, not bolted on later
* **Individualized assessment environments** to keep assignments fair and reduce answer-sharing

Dr. Steven Gold’s conclusion is straightforward: combining **automated coaching** with **direct testing instruments** strengthens both learning and assessment—and the next step is formal research to empirically measure learning impact at scale.
